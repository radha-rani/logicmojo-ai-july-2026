What learning adjustsA model is a function with weights

  • Picture each weight as a dial the model can turn; learning finds the settings.
  • You choose the model's family (its architecture). Within that family, the weights decide the exact function it computes.
  • A straight line can only draw lines; a neural network can bend into almost any shape. Both still learn the same way: by tuning weights.
  • To learn is to search for the weights that make predictions match reality.
Scale is the only differenceThis toy line has two weights. A large language model is the same idea with billions of them, turned by the same procedure you learn today.

What learning adjustsLearning is a guided search

We cannot try every possible setting of the weights. Even two weights give infinite combinations, and real models have millions.

The problemRandom guessing will never find good weight values in a space this large.
The ideaMeasure how wrong the current guess is, then change the weights in the direction that reduces it. Repeat. That needs two tools: a score for wrongness, and a direction.

The score is the loss. The direction is the gradient. The rest of the class is those two.

LossA single number for how wrong

To improve, the model needs a score of how wrong it is right now. That score is the loss. Lower is better; zero would be perfect.

Think of it as Your total miss distance in target practice. Add up how far every shot lands from the bullseye, and one number tells you how badly you are shooting. Every adjustment to your aim is an attempt to shrink it. The model plays the same game against its own errors.

Loss for regressionMean squared error, decoded

$$ L = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left(\hat{y}_i - y_i\right)^2 $$

The default for regressionWhen the label is a number, MSE is the standard loss. Squaring punishes big misses hard, and the curve is smooth, which is exactly what descent needs.
  • \( L \) is the loss, one number for total wrongness.
  • \( n \) is how many examples we score.
  • \( y_i \) is the true answer for example \( i \).
  • \( \hat{y}_i \) is the model's prediction for example \( i \).
  • \( \hat{y}_i - y_i \) is the error on one example.
  • \( (\,\cdot\,)^2 \) makes it positive and weighs big misses more.
  • \( \frac{1}{n}\sum \) averages the per-example errors.

A key distinctionLoss is not the same as a metric

People mix these up. They answer different questions.

Loss: what you optimize
  • Drives training; the thing descent pushes down.
  • Must be smooth so it has a slope to follow.
  • Machine-facing. Examples: MSE, cross-entropy.
Metric: what you report
  • What you and stakeholders actually care about.
  • Can be blunt or non-smooth; that is fine to read.
  • Human-facing. Examples: accuracy, precision, recall, MAE in dollars.
Why the confusionYou often train on one and judge on the other: minimize cross-entropy, report accuracy. MSE is the tricky case, it is a valid loss and a common regression metric, so the two roles overlap there.

Why optimize lossLoss is smooth; accuracy is blunt

  • Accuracy counts right answers. It jumps in steps and does not move when a prediction improves slightly, so it gives no direction to follow.
  • Loss shifts with every tiny change in the weights. That smoothness is exactly what lets us find a downhill direction.

So we minimize a smooth loss in order to improve the metric we care about.

Loss for classificationFrom a score to a probability: the sigmoid

In binary classification we want \( y \approx f(x) \) where \( f(x) \) lands in [0, 1], a real probability. But a weighted sum \( w\cdot x + b \) can be any number. The sigmoid squashes it into range.

$$ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} $$

  • \( z = w\cdot x + b \) is the raw score, any real number.
  • Large positive \( z \) gives \( \sigma \) near 1; large negative near 0; \( z = 0 \) gives 0.5.
  • Read \( \sigma(z) \) as \( p \), the probability the label is 1.

Loss for classificationWhy MSE breaks on classification

Now we hold a probability \( p \) from the sigmoid. Score it against a 0/1 label with MSE and two problems appear.

The penalty is too gentleBeing confidently wrong (predict 0.01 when the truth is 1) costs at most about 1 under MSE. A blunder and a near miss look almost the same.
The signal goes flatNear a confident wrong answer the MSE curve is nearly flat, so its slope is tiny and the model barely learns from its worst mistakes.

We want a loss that makes confident-and-wrong hurt enormously. That loss is cross-entropy.

Loss for classificationBinary cross-entropy, decoded

$$ L = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left[\,y_i\log(p_i) + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\,\right] $$

Only one term survivesIf the truth is 1, the second term is zero and the loss is minus log p. If the truth is 0, the first term is zero and the loss is minus log (1 minus p).
  • \( y_i \) is the true label, 0 or 1.
  • \( p_i \) is the predicted probability that the label is 1.
  • \( \log \) is the natural logarithm.
  • \( -\log(p) \) is small when \( p \) is near 1 and shoots to infinity as \( p \to 0 \).
  • So it rewards confident-correct and savagely punishes confident-wrong.

GradientPicture the loss as a landscape

Every setting of the weights is a position on a hilly surface. The height is the loss. We are hunting for the lowest valley.

The foggy hillside You cannot see the whole landscape through the fog, but you can feel the slope under your feet. Step downhill, again and again, and you reach a valley without ever seeing the map.

GradientRead the slope: which way is downhill

The tangent is the slope at your feet. Slope tilts up to the right means step left; up to the left means step right. Either way, move opposite the slope.

GradientThe gradient points uphill

The gradient of the loss is the direction that increases it fastest, the steepest way up. To reduce the loss, we step the exact opposite way.

One moveCompute the gradient (steepest up), then go the other direction (steepest down). In one dimension that is just the slope; the gradient is the same idea for many weights at once.

GradientMillions of weights, tuned together

$$ \nabla L = \left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1},\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2},\ \dots,\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_m}\right] $$

Reading the symbol \( \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_j} \) is the partial derivative of the loss with respect to one weight: how much \( L \) changes when you nudge only \( w_j \) a little and hold every other weight fixed. It is just a slope, measured along that one weight's axis. The curly \( \partial \) signals "one variable at a time."
A tiny example

$$ L = w_1^{2} + 3\,w_2 $$

  • \( \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = 2w_1 \), treating \( w_2 \) as constant.
  • \( \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} = 3 \), treating \( w_1 \) as constant.
  • At \( (w_1, w_2) = (2, 5) \): \( \nabla L = [\,4,\ 3\,] \).
  • The gradient is a list of slopes, one per weight.
  • One step nudges every weight at once, each opposite its own slope.
  • A large model does this for billions of weights per step.
You do not compute these by handAn automatic method (backpropagation, seen in class 2.3, Neural networks) fills in every slope for you. Today, just hold the picture.

GradientThe update rule, decoded

$$ \theta_{\text{new}} = \theta_{\text{old}} - \eta\,\nabla_\theta L $$

  • \( \theta \) (theta) is one weight (a parameter).
  • grad L is the gradient of the loss for that weight: the steepest-up direction.
  • The minus sign flips it to downhill.
  • \( \eta \) (eta) is the learning rate, how big a step we take.
Read it aloudNew weight equals old weight, minus a small step in the downhill direction.

GradientRolling to the bottom

Each step moves opposite the slope. The steps shrink near the bottom, where the slope flattens out.

Learning rateHow big a step

The learning rate \( \eta \) (eta) sets the step size, and it is the dial you will tune most often.

The two failure modes
  • Too small: learning crawls and takes forever.
  • Too large: you overshoot the valley and can bounce or blow up.
The goal
  • A rate large enough to make real progress each step.
  • Small enough to stay stable and actually settle.

Learning rateSame bowl, three step sizes

Tiny steps inch down one side. Good steps walk to the bottom. Oversized steps leap across the valley and can climb instead of settle.

Learning rateConverge, crawl, or diverge

Bigger is not fasterThe y-axis is loss, the x-axis is steps. Past a point a larger step overshoots and the loss climbs instead of falling. Faster steps are not faster learning if they miss.

How much data per stepFull, stochastic, and mini-batch

Full-batch

Gradient from all data each step. Accurate, but slow and memory-heavy.

Stochastic

One example per step. Fast and noisy; the noise can even help.

Mini-batch

A small group per step. The practical middle, and what class 2.5 (Training in practice) uses.

Step versus epochOne step is one update from one batch. One epoch is a full pass through all the data, usually many steps. You train for several epochs.

How much data per stepSmooth path, or noisy path

The rings are equal-loss contours; the center is the minimum. Full-batch glides in. Stochastic wanders, and that wander can jump it out of shallow dips.

A caveatOne valley, or many?

Real loss landscapes can have several valleys. Descent finds a low point, not always the lowest.

In practiceFor large models this is rarely fatal: most valleys are about as good as each other, and the noise in mini-batch descent helps escape shallow ones. Good enough beats perfect.

The loopPut it together

Repeat the cycle until the loss stops improving, then stop. In class 2.4 (PyTorch fundamentals) you code this exact loop.

BuildWatch descent happen

In the notebook you run gradient descent on a simple curve and plot the point walking to the minimum. You change the learning rate and see it converge, crawl, or blow up, fit a tiny line to data with the same loop, and compare cross-entropy against MSE on a confident-wrong prediction.

The habit to buildPick the loss that fits the task, then let the same descent loop do the work.
