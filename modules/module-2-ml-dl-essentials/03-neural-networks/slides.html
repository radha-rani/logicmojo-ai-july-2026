The neuronA weighted vote, then a gate

A neuron weighs each input by importance, adds them up with a bias, then passes the total through an activation.

  • Weigh the inputs, each by its own weight.
  • Add a bias to shift the threshold up or down.
  • Pass the total through a gate, the activation.
Think of it as A hiring committee. Each interviewer (an input) carries a weight based on how much their opinion counts, the chair adds a baseline lean (the bias), and a final rule (the gate) decides whether the candidate passes. Change the weights and the same committee reaches a different verdict.

The neuronWhat one neuron does

Inputs come in weighted, get summed with a bias into the pre-activation \( z \), and pass through the activation \( f \) to give the output \( a \). That is the entire neuron.

The neuronThe formula, decoded

$$ z = \sum_i w_i x_i + b, \qquad a = f(z) $$

Read it aloudWeigh the inputs, add a bias, then pass the total through a gate.
  • \( x_i \) are the inputs, the features.
  • \( w_i \) are the weights, how much each input matters, the same weights the loop in class 2.2 (How models learn) adjusts.
  • \( \sum_i w_i x_i \) is a dot product, a weighted vote.
  • \( b \) is the bias, a fixed shift.
  • \( z \) is the pre-activation total; \( f \) is the activation; \( a \) is the output that flows on.

ArchitectureFrom one neuron to layers

Put several neurons side by side to form a layer, then feed one layer's outputs into the next. A network has three kinds of layer.

  • Input layer: one node per feature. It just holds the input; no computation happens here.
  • Hidden layers: the computing neurons in the middle. There can be one or many.
  • Output layer: the final neurons that produce the prediction.
Fully connected (dense)Every neuron in a layer connects to every node in the layer before it. Such layers are called dense layers.

ArchitectureHow we count layers

By convention we count only the layers that compute, the ones with learnable weights. The input layer holds numbers and does no computation, so it is not counted.

One hidden layer
  • Input, then one hidden layer, then output.
  • Two computing layers, so a "2-layer network", also called a "1-hidden-layer network".
Two hidden layers
  • Input, two hidden layers, then output.
  • Three computing layers, a "3-layer network", or "2-hidden-layer network".
Why it mattersWhen a paper says "a 3-layer MLP", it means three layers that compute, not three circles of nodes. The input layer never counts.

ActivationsWhy a nonlinearity is required

Stack layers with no activation and they collapse into a single straight line, no matter how many you add.

$$ y = W_2(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2 = W' x + b' $$

See it in numbersLet layer 1 be \( a = 2x + 1 \) and layer 2 be \( y = 3a - 2 \). Substitute: \( y = 3(2x+1) - 2 = 6x + 1 \). Two linear layers are just one line, \( 6x + 1 \). A nonlinear gate between them is what lets the network bend.

ActivationsWhat the nonlinearity buys you

Bend the space

With nonlinear activations a network becomes a universal function approximator: given enough neurons it can bend to match almost any continuous relationship in the data. Straight lines cannot.

Decide what fires

The gate decides which neurons pass their signal on and which stay quiet, so the network becomes selective about what information flows forward.

Keep values in range

Some gates, like sigmoid and tanh, squash the output into a small band. That keeps numbers from blowing up and steadies training.

Allow learning

The gates are differentiable, so gradients can flow back through them. That is exactly what backpropagation, later this class, needs to work.

ActivationsReLU: keep the positives

$$ f(z) = \max(0, z) $$

  • Keep positive values exactly as they are.
  • Set every negative value to zero.
  • A simple on/off-style gate with a straight positive arm.
  • The default in hidden layers, because it is cheap and trains fast.

ActivationsSigmoid: squash into 0 to 1

$$ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} $$

  • \( e \) is a fixed constant near 2.718.
  • \( e^{-z} \) shrinks toward 0 as \( z \) grows, and grows large as \( z \) drops.
  • The whole expression squashes any number into the range 0 to 1.
  • The same squasher from class 2.2 (How models learn); that is why its output reads as a probability.

Activationstanh, and the wider family

$$ \tanh(z) = \frac{e^{z} - e^{-z}}{e^{z} + e^{-z}} $$

  • An S-curve like sigmoid, but centered at 0 with range -1 to 1.
  • Zero-centered outputs often train a little better than sigmoid.
  • It shares sigmoid's flat tails, so it can still stall gradients.
Many more existYou will also meet Leaky ReLU, ELU, Maxout, GELU, and Swish. They are all variations on the same idea, a simple nonlinear gate. ReLU and its relatives dominate hidden layers today.

ActivationsWhy ReLU is the default

For large positive or negative \( z \), the sigmoid and tanh curves flatten out, and a flat curve has almost zero slope.

The vanishing gradientNear-zero slope means a near-zero gradient, so learning crawls to a halt in deep stacks of sigmoids. ReLU keeps a full, constant slope for every positive input, which is why hidden layers default to it.

The neuronA neuron by the numbers

Now that the gates are defined, put real numbers through one neuron. Inputs \( x = (2, 3) \), weights \( w = (0.5, -1) \), bias \( b = 1 \).

Step 1: the weighted sum

\( z = 0.5\cdot 2 + (-1)\cdot 3 + 1 = -1 \)

Step 2: the activation
  • ReLU: \( \max(0, -1) = 0 \), the neuron stays quiet.
  • Sigmoid: \( \sigma(-1) \approx 0.27 \), a soft partial signal.
Same machine, different gateThe weighted sum never changes. Swapping the activation only changes how the total is squashed on its way out, which is why the choice of gate matters.

Output layerSoftmax: scores into probabilities

$$ \text{softmax}(z)_j = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_k e^{z_k}} $$

Read it aloudExponentiate each score, then share the total out as probabilities that add to 1.
  • \( z_j \) is the raw score, the logit, for class \( j \).
  • \( e^{z_j} \) makes it positive and amplifies larger scores.
  • Dividing by the sum over all classes \( k \) normalizes them to add to 1.
  • For just two classes a single sigmoid does the same job; softmax is the many-class generalization.

Output layerSoftmax by the numbers

Logits \( z = (2, 1, 0) \).

  • Exponentiate: \( e^2, e^1, e^0 = 7.39,\ 2.72,\ 1.00 \).
  • Sum: \( 7.39 + 2.72 + 1.00 = 11.11 \).
  • Divide: \( 0.665,\ 0.245,\ 0.090 \), which add to 1.

Output layerCross-entropy: the classification loss

$$ L = -\sum_{k} y_k \log(p_k) $$

  • \( p_k \) is the model's predicted probability for class \( k \), from softmax.
  • \( y_k \) is the true distribution, a one-hot vector: 1 for the correct class, 0 for the rest.
  • It measures how far the predicted distribution \( p \) sits from the target \( y \).
  • Since \( y \) is one-hot, every term is 0 except the true class, so it collapses to \( L = -\log(p_{\text{true}}) \).
Worked example
  • Target (one-hot): \( y = (0,\ 0,\ 1) \).
  • Predicted: \( p = (0.1,\ 0.2,\ 0.7) \).
  • \( L = -(0\cdot\log 0.1 + 0\cdot\log 0.2 + 1\cdot\log 0.7) \)
  • \( \;\;= -\log(0.7) \approx 0.36 \).
You have seen its twinThis generalizes the binary cross-entropy from class 2.2 (How models learn): softmax plus cross-entropy is the multi-class version of sigmoid plus binary cross-entropy.

Forward passRun the input through, layer by layer

The forward pass is the neuron computation repeated for every layer in order. Each layer does its weighted sum and activation, hands its output to the next layer, and a prediction comes out the far end.

One directionInputs enter on the left, flow through the hidden layers, and leave as a prediction on the right. Nothing loops back yet; that backward flow is backpropagation, coming next.

Forward passA forward pass, by hand

Hidden weights \( W^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} \), output weights \( W^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \), all biases 0, ReLU on the hidden layer. Each row of \( W^{(1)} \) is one hidden neuron's weights.

  • h1: \( z = 0.5\cdot1 + 0.5\cdot2 = 1.5 \), ReLU keeps it, so \( a_1 = 1.5 \).
  • h2: \( z = 0\cdot1 + 0.5\cdot2 = 1.0 \), so \( a_2 = 1.0 \).
  • output: \( \hat{y} = 1\cdot1.5 + 2\cdot1.0 = 3.5 \).

TrainingBackpropagation, at a glance

Training a network is the loop from class 2.2 (How models learn) applied to every weight at once.

  • Run the forward pass and compute the loss.
  • Backpropagation passes the error backward through the layers with the chain rule, giving each weight its own gradient.
  • The update rule then steps every weight a little downhill.
Not a new algorithmBackprop is just an efficient way to get the gradients. The step itself is still the update rule from class 2.2 (How models learn).

TrainingBackprop assigns the blame

After a wrong prediction, the error is handed back layer by layer. Each weight learns how much it contributed to the mistake, and therefore which way to move.

Think of it asA relay team that drops the baton at the finish. To improve, they trace the handoffs backward: the last runner's share of the fault, then the one before, and so on down the line. Each runner gets a fair share of the blame and adjusts. Backprop assigns that blame to every weight, precisely, using the chain rule.
You will not do this by handIn practice PyTorch and TensorFlow build the graph during the forward pass and compute every gradient automatically. One call, loss.backward(), does all of it. We rely on that from class 2.4 (PyTorch fundamentals) on and never hand-derive gradients again.
